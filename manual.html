What Kite is

Kite is an app for iPhone, iPad and Android for talking to AI you control. There are two ways to use it, and they are genuinely different things.

This manual is written for iPhone and iPad. The Android app connects the same way — a helper on your computer, pairing, your own model. Siri, Shortcuts and the Action button are iPhone and iPad features; on Android, notifications and sharing are managed in Android's own Settings. Android also has a Check Tailscale button in Kite's Settings: it tells you whether Tailscale is installed and whether your agent's address answers, so you can tell a network problem from a Kite one.

  • A model, directly. Any OpenAI-compatible endpoint with your own key — or no key, if the model runs on your own computer. You get a plain chat: fast, private, nothing to install anywhere.
  • An agent. Hermes, the open-source agent from Nous Research, running on your Mac or a server. It searches, reads, runs commands, remembers, and touches your actual files. Kite shows every step as it happens.

You can switch between them whenever you like, and each keeps its own settings. Start with a model if you want something working in a minute; add an agent when you want work done rather than questions answered.

Talking to a model

Settings → Connect to → A model directly. The endpoint is a plain text field and the API key is optional, which is what makes both of these possible:

  • A model on your own machine. Settings → Find models on my network looks for Ollama, LM Studio, llama.cpp or Jan on your wi-fi and fills in everything — address, port and model. No key, because a server on your own network does not ask for one. Ollama needs OLLAMA_HOST set to be reachable from anything but the machine it runs on.
  • A hosted provider. Buttons fill in OpenRouter and Nous Portal; any other OpenAI-compatible service works by typing its base URL — Groq, Together, DeepInfra and so on. Paste your key, pick a model.
  • A model on your own computer. Point it at http://<address>:11434/v1 for Ollama, or LM Studio's server address, and leave the key blank.

⚠️ Ollama only listens to itself by default. On 127.0.0.1 your phone cannot reach it. Start it on an address your phone can see:

OLLAMA_HOST=<your-tailnet-address>:11434 ollama serve

Use your private-network address, not 0.0.0.0. Ollama has no authentication at all, so binding it to everything offers your models to every device on whatever network you happen to be on — including hotel and café wi-fi.

A hosted provider works from anywhere with nothing else set up. A model on your own machine needs a private path to it, which is the next section.

Talking to an agent

An agent is the part no other chat client can offer, and it needs two things: the agent itself running on a machine of yours, and Kite Helper beside it so your phone never holds the agent's key.

Set it up on a Mac or on a server — Kite connects to either, and the difference is only which machine its tools act on.

Install Tailscale if you are reaching your own machine. You do not need it for a hosted provider.

  • A hosted provider: not needed at all — it works anywhere, like any other app.
  • Your own machine, at home: works without it, over your wifi.
  • Your own machine, anywhere else: needs it. There is no other route to your Mac from a café or a train — that applies to a local model just as much as to an agent.
  • A server, always: needs it. Your phone is never on the same network as a server.

Tailscale is free for personal use, takes a few minutes, and needs installing on both your phone and the machine you are reaching — signed into the same account, so they can see each other.

⚠️ The alternative — opening your machine to the internet — is a bad trade either way. An agent can run commands on the machine it lives on, so a leaked token is a shell on your Mac or your server; a local model server such as Ollama has no authentication at all, so anyone who reaches it can use it. Kite is built assuming you will not do this.

An agent on your Mac

Best if your files and tools are on that Mac. It has to be awake for your phone to reach it.

  1. Install Hermes and get it running with its API server enabled.
  2. Install Kite Helper, a small menu-bar app. It holds your agent's key so your phone never has to.
  3. Open Kite on your phone and scan the QR code from the helper's menu.
  4. Install Tailscale on the Mac and the phone, unless you only ever intend to use Kite at home.

That is the whole setup. You are not asked for an address, a certificate or an account, because the QR carries everything.

An agent on a server

Best if you would rather not leave a Mac on. The agent is always available, and its tools act on the server rather than on your Mac.

curl -O https://kiteagent.app/downloads/install-hermes-server.sh
bash install-hermes-server.sh

It checks the machine first, installs the agent and helper, starts both, verifies the whole chain and prints a pairing link. Roughly 370 MB of disk and about 190 MB of memory while answering — it runs on a 1 GB server, though without much to spare.

⚠️ Do not open the helper's port to the internet as a shortcut around Tailscale. A pairing token would be the only thing between a stranger and an agent that can run commands on your server.

Pairing, and what happens when you leave home

The pairing code carries two addresses: your machine's local network address and its private network (Tailscale) address. Kite prefers the local one at home because it is direct, and falls back to the private one when you are out.

This means a Mac agent works on your home wifi with no Tailscale at all. Away from home you need it — there is no other route.

If your Mac changes address — a new DHCP lease, or moving between wifi and a hotspot — Kite races both known addresses to find it again. If it ever cannot, re-scanning the QR always fixes it.

More than one machine

Kite remembers every machine you connect it to. Settings → Connections lists them, and switching is one tap — no scanning, no re-pairing. Each keeps its own address, its own credential and its own chosen model, so moving between a Mac at home and a server elsewhere does not cost you a setup each time.

Adding a server needs no code at all. Settings → Connections → Add a machine by address, then type its name on your tailnet — my-server, or its address. The machine hands the phone what it needs once it can see you are on the tailnet, which is a stronger check than a code copied off a screen. That is also the only way to pair a machine with no screen to show a code on.

Add a machine by address works for a Mac too, which matters when you are not sitting in front of it. Scanning the code is still there when you are.

Choosing a model

Using a model on your own machine. Point the endpoint at it and leave the key blank — for Ollama that is http://<address>:11434/v1. ⚠️ Ollama listens only on 127.0.0.1 by default, so start it bound to an address your phone can reach: OLLAMA_HOST=<your-tailnet-address>:11434 ollama serve. Binding it to 0.0.0.0 instead would expose it to every device on whatever network you are on, and Ollama has no authentication at all.

On Android, an address on the public internet has to start with https://; Kite will not send your messages to it over plain http://. Your own machines — localhost, your home network, your tailnet and short names like mac-mini — keep working over http://.

Kite lists whatever you are connected to — your agent's models, or your provider's, or the ones on your own machine — with free ones badged. Your choice is locked to each conversation, so a thread keeps answering with the model it started with.

⚠️ Free models run out faster than you expect. In plain chat one message is one request, but every step an agent takes is its own request — one question that reads a few files can be twenty — and a free key's daily allowance is small.

Free models also change. Providers retire the free tier of a model without notice. When that happens, Kite remembers it for three days, switches you to another free model and tells you; send your message again. It only ever switches from a free model to a free model, so a paid choice of yours is never replaced.

When it runs out you will see 429 rate limit exceeded: free-models-per-day. The allowance resets at midnight UTC. Adding 10 credits at your provider raises it to 1,000 requests a day, and you carry on using free models.

⚠️ If you pair with a different agent, check your model. Agents are configured differently — a model your Mac can serve may not exist on your server. Kite switches you to something the new agent offers, but only when the model is genuinely absent, not when it is present and out of quota.

The menu

Kite opens straight into a conversation, because you open it to talk. Everything that is not a conversation lives in a menu.

  • Open it with the menu button (☰) at the top left. On iPhone you can also swipe in from the left edge of the screen; on Android, swipe right across the screen (the very edge is Android’s own Back gesture).
  • What is in it: Chats, Routines and Status (the last two only when you are talking to an agent), and Settings. A line at the bottom says how the connection is doing: Connected with the model name, Working…, or Can’t reach your agent.
  • The gear is gone. Settings is now in the menu. If you are looking for the gear or a back arrow, tap ☰ instead.
  • Back to your chats from a conversation: on iPhone, swipe right across the conversation, or open the menu and tap Chats. On Android the system back gesture does it.

Everyday use

  • Watch it work. Searching, reading, running commands — steps appear as they happen, not just the answer.
  • Send it anything. Share a link or a document from any app. PDFs are read on your phone, scans included.
  • Photos need a model that can see. Share a picture to an agent and it goes through — but only a vision-capable model can do anything with it. On one that cannot, the reply will politely tell you it sees no image. If that happens, switch model rather than assuming the photo did not arrive. One caveat: Hermes stores the picture in the transcript as a placeholder, so reopening that conversation later shows the words, not the image.
  • Search inside a conversation. Pull down in a thread; matches highlight and the arrows step through them.
  • Pick up anywhere. With an agent, conversations live on it, so your desktop and terminal see the same threads. Talking to a model directly, they live on the phone and nowhere else — the list shows them under On this phone.

⚠️ Each agent keeps its own conversations. Pairing with a different one shows you that agent's threads, not a merged list — nothing syncs between two agents.

While an agent works, each step appears as its own row — a search, a command, a file read. Tap a row and it shows what was actually asked for: the command, the path, the URL. Collapsed it stays out of the way; open it is evidence rather than a claim.

Routines

A routine is a prompt your agent runs by itself on a schedule — “every weekday at 8, summarise the overnight news”. Hermes calls them cron jobs. They run on your agent, whether or not Kite is open. Routines appear in the menu when you are talking to an agent, and need a recent version of Hermes; an older one says so on the screen.

The list puts running routines first, soonest next, then paused ones, then any that stopped or finished. Each row shows the name, the schedule, and when it runs next or last ran. A last run that failed is shown in red.

A routine’s page shows its schedule, next and last run, how many times it has run, what it asks, and the last problem if there was one. From there:

  • Pause and Resume stop and restart it.
  • Run now asks first, then your agent runs it at its next check, usually within a minute. It can use its tools, exactly as it does on schedule. It is offered for a running routine; a paused one has Resume instead, and a finished or stopped one cannot be run again.
  • Edit opens the same form as a new routine, filled in. Only what you change is sent.
  • Delete asks first. It cannot be undone.

Making a routine: tap + at the top right of the list. Give it a name, say what it should ask your agent, and choose when it runs: every few minutes or hours, every day, weekdays, every week, or once. Custom takes Hermes’s own wording, for example every 2h, 0 9 * * 1-5, weekdays at 9am or in 30m. You can also stop it after a number of runs.

⚠️ A routine runs with no one to ask. The prompt is sent each time, and your agent can use its tools, run commands and read files, as it does when you chat. Write it as you would for something you will not be watching.

  • Times are on your agent’s clock, the time zone of the computer it runs on, which may not be your phone’s.
  • Where the answer goes. A routine made in Kite keeps its answer on your agent’s computer. Kite shows when a routine ran and whether it worked, not the answer itself. Editing never changes where an answer goes, so a routine you set up elsewhere to deliver to, say, Telegram keeps doing so.
  • If your agent refuses a schedule or a prompt, Kite shows its message under the form so you can fix it.

Status

The Status screen in the menu shows how your agent is doing and how much it has been used. Pull down to refresh. It appears only when you are talking to an agent.

  • Agent. Whether it is running, or Working on 1 thing now, the Hermes version if your agent reports one, and the model it is set to.
  • Today, on the agent. Requests, tokens, and how long the slowest 5% took.
  • Usage. Today, the last 7 days and the last 30 days: how many chats, how many tokens in and out, and a cost if Hermes reports one (it is Hermes’s own estimate). Below that, where the use came from — Kite, your routines, Telegram, the terminal.

⚠️ “Today, on the agent” counts less than Usage does, and can read zero. It counts only the chats and runs sent to your agent through its API, such as from Kite. Routines, Telegram and the agent’s own terminal are not in it, and it starts again from zero each day and whenever your agent restarts. Usage is the figure for how much your agent was used.

Usage is worked out from your agent’s latest 200 conversations, and a conversation counts on the day it was last active. If those 200 do not reach back 30 days, the screen says so and the 30-day figure may be higher than shown. An older Hermes that does not report its own status says that instead, and Usage still shows.

Siri and Shortcuts

Kite answers Siri without opening. Say “Ask Kite”, then your question; it goes to whichever connection Kite is currently pointed at, waits for the reply, and hands it back to Siri. Ask your agent whether the build passed while you are walking to the car, and you never touch the phone.

Whether Siri speaks the answer or just shows it is an iOS setting, not something Kite controls: Settings ▸ Siri ▸ Siri Responses. If you expected it read aloud and got text, that is where to look.

“Send to Kite” is the other half: it hands the message over and returns immediately, which is right for anything long. The run continues on your machine and the reply arrives as a notification.

Both appear in the Shortcuts app under Kite, so you can put them in an automation, on the Home Screen, or on the Action button. “Ask Kite” returns the reply as text, so a shortcut can send it on somewhere — into a note, a message, anything.

The spoken answer is trimmed to something Siri can sensibly read. A shortcut receives the whole reply, however long.

The Ask widget

Add the “Ask your agent” widget from Kite’s entry in the widget list. On iPhone it comes as a small Home Screen widget and in Lock Screen sizes; on Android it is a Home Screen widget. Tap it and Kite opens on a new chat with the cursor in the message field. It also shows whether your agent is reachable, its next routine and how many have failed, as of the last time Kite ran. It makes no network request of its own, and it never shows what your agent said.

Apple Watch

Ask your agent from your wrist. Your iPhone does the asking: the watch passes your question to Kite on the phone, and the phone sends it, so keep the phone nearby. The watch never talks to your agent or holds any of your keys. This is an iPhone feature; it needs watchOS 10 or newer.

  • Get it. The watch app comes with Kite. If it is not on your watch, open the Watch app on your iPhone, find Kite, and turn on Show App on Apple Watch.
  • Ask. Open Kite on the watch, tap the field and speak. The answer appears on the wrist. Cancel stops waiting. A very long answer is trimmed for the small screen.
  • If it takes a while. After about 40 seconds the watch says it is still working, and the answer arrives on the wrist when it is ready.
  • Your chats. Swipe left from the ask screen for your recent chats. Open one to read it. The newest few are ready at once, and the others load when you open them, which needs the phone nearby. Reply carries on in the same conversation on your phone.
  • A complication for your watch face puts Kite one tap away.

It uses the same connection as Siri and the share sheet, so the same rules apply. Your free trial has to be running or Kite unlocked. Talking to a model directly, send one message in Kite first, so it can tell you where your messages go before anything leaves your phone on its own. And if your agent stops to ask permission to run a command, the watch cannot answer: ask that one in Kite on your iPhone.

Notifications and the Lock Screen

A long task tells you when it is done, and a Live Activity shows what it is doing while you wait.

⚠️ Notifications only arrive if you leave. They fire when your phone disconnects while the agent is still working — which is the situation they exist for. Watch a reply arrive on screen and nothing is sent, which is correct and looks exactly like a broken feature when you are testing it deliberately.

Open Kite once after pairing with an agent. It registers for notifications then, so an agent you have paired with but never opened cannot notify you.

When something goes wrong

“Hermes is not logged into Nous Portal”

Your agent has no provider configured and is falling back to one it has never signed into. Pin a provider in ~/.hermes/config.yaml, or pick a model in Kite that the agent can actually serve. The server installer does this for you.

A local model does not answer

Ollama binds 127.0.0.1 by default, which your phone cannot reach — start it on your private-network address instead (see Talking to a model). Check the address ends in /v1, leave the API key empty, and make sure your phone is on the same private network as that machine.

An empty reply, or the first message of a new chat fails

On a self-installed agent, check API_SERVER_MODEL_NAME in ~/.hermes/.env. Despite its description it is sent upstream as the model id when a conversation has no model chosen, so the stock value is rejected by every provider. It must be a real model name.

A red dot, or “couldn't reach your agent”

  • Is the machine awake, and the thing you are talking to actually running — the agent, or your local model server?
  • Away from home — is Tailscale on, on both the phone and the machine?
  • On a guest wifi network? Many routers stop devices seeing each other.
  • Still stuck: re-scan the pairing QR. It always resets the address.

Commands run, then nothing

Usually the model refusing rather than the agent stopping — most often a daily quota. The thread will say which; if it says nothing, the agent's own log is the place to look.

No notifications

Open Kite once while connected to that agent, then test by sending something slow and locking the phone. If you stay on the screen, nothing will arrive by design.

Kite is missing from the share sheet

Restart the phone. iOS remembers which apps handle which kinds of content and does not always notice when an update adds more — so after updating, Kite can be absent for photos or documents until the phone has been restarted once. If it is still missing, check More at the end of the app row: new extensions are sometimes listed there switched off.

What leaves your devices

  • Conversations stay where you put them. With an agent, in its own database on your machine; talking to a model directly, on the phone and nowhere else. There is no account and no sign-up.
  • Your messages go to whichever model provider you chose, under your own key. Local storage, remote inference — worth being clear-eyed about.
  • Your agent's key never reaches your phone. The helper holds it; your phone gets a pairing token, revocable on its own.
  • Notifications carry no message content — only that a reply arrived. Your phone then fetches it over your own private network. This is the one part that involves a server of ours: waking an iPhone needs a signing key that cannot be given out, so a small relay holds it and is sent your notification token and nothing else, kept for 90 days and refreshed whenever you open the app.

The full privacy policy is short, because there is little to describe.

Still stuck?

Ask on the support forum. If it is a connection problem, Settings → the connection log is the only place that shows the phone's side of things, and pasting the last few lines usually answers it in one go.
